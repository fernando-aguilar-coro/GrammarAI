Hello there! Today, we are diving into the world of three-part phrasal verbs. These are fascinating because they consist of a base verb followed by two particles—an adverb and a preposition. Because they are inseparable, the object must always come at the very end. Think of it like a fixed chain: you have the verb, then the adverb, then the preposition, and finally, the object.

Let’s look at three essential examples you will use all the time.

First, we have look forward to, pronounced look-for-ward-too. This means to anticipate something with excitement. A very important rule here is that the word to is a preposition, not an infinitive marker. This means you must follow it with a noun or a verb ending in ing. For example, instead of saying I look forward to see you, you should say, I look forward to seeing you.

Next is run out of, pronounced run-out-uv. This means you have used up your entire supply of something. You might say, We ran out of coffee this morning. Remember, the object—in this case, the coffee—must come right after the word of.

Finally, we have keep up with, pronounced keep-up-with. This is used when you are trying to maintain the same pace as someone else, whether you are running, studying, or following the news. For instance, He reads daily to keep up with industry news.

Now, let’s talk about how to avoid some common mistakes. First, never try to separate these verbs. You cannot say I ran the milk out of; you must keep the three parts together and say I ran out of milk. Second, don't forget the second particle. If you just say I need to keep with the class, you lose the meaning. You must include the word up to make it keep up with.

The biggest trap students fall into is with look forward to. Remember, that little word to is a magnet for a gerund, which is a verb ending in ing. So, keep practicing these as a single unit, and you will sound much more natural and fluent in no time.
