Hello and welcome! Today, we are going to master distributive quantifiers. These are special words we use to point to individual members of a group rather than the group as a whole. They help us show how things are distributed, separated, or paired up. 

Let us start with each and every. 

Each, which we pronounce as each, looks at individual items in a group of two or more, one by one. For example, we say, Each student received a grade. 

Every, pronounced as ev-ree, is used for three or more items, looking at them as a collective whole. For example, Every room was thoroughly cleaned. Notice that every must always be followed by a singular countable noun.

Next, we have three quantifiers used specifically for pairs: both, either, and neither. 

Both, pronounced as bowth, links two items together in an affirmative way. It is followed by a plural noun, as in, Both candidates performed well. 

Either, which you can pronounce as eye-ther or ee-ther, refers to one or the other of two choices. For example, You can take either road. 

Neither, pronounced as nigh-ther or nee-ther, means not one and not the other of two choices. For example, Neither option is acceptable.

Now, let us look at some crucial grammar rules to keep your English sounding natural and correct. 

First, let us talk about verb agreement. The words each, every, either, and neither always take a singular verb when they are the subject. Only both takes a plural verb. 

Second, pay attention to the preposition of. If you want to follow these quantifiers with a pronoun or a specific noun phrase, you must use of. For example, you should say, Each of the participants completed the survey, not, Each the participants. Note that every cannot be used this way; you would need to say every one of instead.

Third, remember that each, either, and neither can stand alone as pronouns. For instance, you can say, I offered them two drinks, but neither was cold. However, the word every can never stand alone. Instead of saying, I asked them and every said yes, you must say, everyone said yes.

To wrap up, let us avoid a few common mistakes. 

Never use every when talking about only two things. If you have two hands, you would say each hand, not every hand. 

Also, watch your verb agreement with of phrases. Even though books is plural, we say, Each of the books is interesting, because the true subject, each, is singular. 

Finally, do not pair either or neither directly with a plural noun. Say, neither student, or, neither of the students, but never, neither students.
