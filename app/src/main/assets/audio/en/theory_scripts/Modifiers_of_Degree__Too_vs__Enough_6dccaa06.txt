Hello everyone! Today, we are mastering two super common words in English: too and enough. These are modifiers of degree, which means they tell us about the intensity of an adjective, adverb, or noun. While they might seem simple, placing them in the wrong spot is a very common mistake. Let's make sure you get it right every time!

First, let's look at the word too, pronounced /tuː/. Too means there is an excess of something—more than is needed, wanted, or comfortable. Because of this, it almost always carries a negative feeling or describes an obstacle.

When you use too with an adjective or an adverb, it always goes before the word. For example, you can say, "The coffee is too hot," meaning the temperature exceeds what you can drink. Or, "He ran too quickly." 

If you want to add an action using a to-infinitive, you can say, "It is too heavy to lift." Here, the weight prevents you from lifting it. When working with nouns, remember to add much or many. For instance, "There is too much noise to study."

Now, let me introduce enough, pronounced /ɪˈnʌf/. Unlike too, enough indicates sufficiency—it means you have just the right amount or degree of something. 

Here is the tricky part about enough: its position changes depending on what word you are modifying!

When you use it with adjectives or adverbs, enough comes after the word. For example, "She is tall enough." Or, combining it with an action, "He is old enough to drive." 

However, when you use enough with a noun, it goes before the noun. For example, "We have enough time."

So, let's summarize that golden rule: too comes before adjectives and adverbs. Enough comes after adjectives and adverbs, but before nouns!

Let's cover a few common pitfalls to avoid. 

First, don't confuse too with very. Saying "The soup is very hot" simply intensifies the heat, but saying "The soup is too hot" means it is a problem and you cannot eat it.

Second, watch your word order with adjectives. Placing enough before an adjective, like saying "enough warm," is incorrect. Always place the adjective first, as in "warm enough."

And finally, if you want to specify who is affected by an action, insert for plus a pronoun before the infinitive. For example, "It is too heavy for me to lift," or "Is it warm enough for you?"

Keep practicing these rules in your daily speaking, and you will sound more natural in no time. Great job today!
