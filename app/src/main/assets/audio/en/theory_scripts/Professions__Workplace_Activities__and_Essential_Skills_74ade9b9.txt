Welcome to today's lesson on professional terminology and workplace communication. Communicating clearly at work requires a strong command of three core areas: your profession, your daily workplace activities, and your essential skills. 

Let us start by looking at common professions and the roles they play. 

Take a software engineer, pronounced software engineer. Their primary activity involves writing and debugging code, which demands strong analytical thinking. 

Next, consider a project manager. Notice the stress in project manager. They focus on overseeing project timelines, a role that requires leadership and keen organizational skills. 

Then we have a financial analyst, pronounced financial analyst. They spend their days evaluating investment data, relying heavily on numerical literacy. 

Finally, a human resources specialist works on recruiting and training staff. For this role, interpersonal communication is absolutely essential.

When describing what you do every day, dynamic action verbs bring your communication to life. Remember your grammar foundations: use the present simple for daily routines, and the present continuous for tasks happening right now. 

Here are four high-impact workplace verbs to add to your vocabulary:

First is collaborate, pronounced collaborate, which means working jointly with others on a project. 

Second is delegate, pronounced delegate, meaning to entrust a task or responsibility to a team member. 

Third is implement, pronounced implement, which means putting a plan or decision into action. 

And fourth is streamline, pronounced streamline, which means improving an organization or process to make it more efficient.

Beyond these actions, every professional operates with a mix of hard skills and soft skills. Hard skills are technical, teachable abilities that can be measured, such as coding, data analysis, or speaking a foreign language. Soft skills, on the other hand, are personal attributes that govern how you interact with others, such as adaptability, emotional intelligence, and conflict resolution. In interviews and resumes, a healthy balance of both is critical.

To sound truly professional, watch out for a few common pitfalls. 

A frequent mistake involves prepositions. When you describe your duties using the phrase responsible for, you must always follow it with a gerund, ending in i-n-g. Never say responsible for manage. Instead, say, I am responsible for managing the team.

Also, avoid overly generic verbs like do or make. Instead of saying you did a project, elevate your language by saying you executed a plan, formulated a strategy, or coordinated an event. 

Practice using these terms today, and watch your professional confidence soar.
