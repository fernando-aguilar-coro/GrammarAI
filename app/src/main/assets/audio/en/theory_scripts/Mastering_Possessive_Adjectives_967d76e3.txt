Welcome to today's lesson. Today, we are mastering possessive adjectives. These are the essential words we use to show who owns or has something. Unlike some pronouns, possessive adjectives never stand alone. They always come right before the noun they describe.

Let's walk through each form step by step, starting with the singular ones.

When talking about yourself, the pronoun I becomes my. For example: "My car is fast."

For the person you are speaking to, you becomes your, pronounced your. For instance: "Is this your book?"

When referring to a male owner, he becomes his, pronounced his. For example: "His dog is loud."

For a female owner, she becomes her, pronounced her. As in: "Her presentation was clear."

And for an animal, an idea, or an object, it becomes its, pronounced its. For example: "The cat chased its tail."

Now let's look at the plural forms.

When speaking about a group that includes yourself, we becomes our, pronounced our. For example: "Our team won the match."

Finally, when referring to other people or plural items, they becomes their, pronounced their. For instance: "Their house is large."

Now, let's look at a golden rule of English grammar: the possessive adjective always agrees with the owner, not the item being possessed. This is especially important if your native language assigns grammatical gender to objects. In English, if Mary owns a truck, we say "her truck", because Mary is female. We never change the adjective based on the object. 

Also, remember that possessive adjectives always sit directly before the noun phrase, like saying "our brand new table."

Before we finish, let's look at three common traps to avoid.

First, never use an apostrophe with possessive adjectives. The word its, spelled I-T-S with no apostrophe, shows ownership, as in "the dog wagged its tail." If you write I-T-apostrophe-S, that means "it is" or "it has."

Second, watch out for words that sound the same. The possessive your sounds exactly like you're, which means you are. Similarly, their sounds identical to there and they're. 

Finally, never substitute a subject pronoun for a possessive adjective. Avoid saying things like "I lost I keys." Always say: "I lost my keys."
