Hello everyone, and welcome back! Today, we are exploring a fantastic area of English grammar: modal verbs of probability and deduction. We use these verbs when we want to express how sure we are about a situation based on the evidence we have.

Think of these modals as existing on a scale of certainty, ranging from logical certainty to just a small possibility.

At the very top, when you feel about ninety-five percent certain that something is positive and true, use must, pronounced /mʌst/. For example, if you see the lights on in a house, you can say: She must be at home.

When you are ninety-five percent certain that something is impossible, use can't, pronounced /kænt/. If the house is completely dark, you would say: She can't be at home.

Moving down to a fifty-fifty chance, we have could, pronounced /kʊd/, and may, pronounced /meɪ/. These show a reasonable, present possibility.

Finally, for a weaker or more remote possibility—around thirty to forty percent certainty—we use might, pronounced /maɪt/. For instance: It might rain later, even though the sky is mostly clear.

Now, let's look at how to build these sentences.

When you are making a deduction about the present moment, the rule is simple: use your chosen modal verb followed directly by the base form of the main verb. For example: She must be at home.

When you are guessing about the past, however, you need to use a modal followed by have and the past participle of the verb. So, if you notice her coat is missing and you are speculating about what happened earlier, you would say: She must have left early.

To sound like a natural speaker, keep these common mistakes in mind.

First, avoid using must not when you mean that something is logically impossible. English speakers use can't for negative deductions. You should say, He can't be the thief, rather than, He must not be the thief.

Second, never drop the word have when talking about the past. Do not say, She might left. Always remember to say: She might have left.

And lastly, try not to use the word can for present deductions. Instead, stick to could, may, or might when speculating about right now.

Mastering these modal verbs will make your spoken English much more nuanced and natural. Keep practicing these structures, and I will see you in our next lesson!
