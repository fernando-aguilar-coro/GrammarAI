Hello and welcome! Today, we are mastering the third conditional, a fantastic structure for talking about the past, expressing regrets, and imagining how things could have turned out differently. We use it to describe past counterfactuals, which simply means situations that never actually happened and their imagined past results.

Let us look at how to build it. A complete third conditional sentence has two parts: the if-clause and the main clause. 

For the if-clause, you start with if, followed by your subject, the auxiliary had, and the past participle of the verb. For example: If she had studied harder. In casual spoken English, notice how she had naturally contracts to she'd.

Next comes the main clause, which describes the imagined result. Here, we use the subject, followed by would have, and the past participle. For instance: she would have passed the exam. In natural connected speech, would have often sounds like would've. 

Putting both clauses together, we get: If she had studied harder, she would have passed the exam. But of course, the reality is that she did not study harder, and she did not pass.

You do not always have to use would have. You can easily swap in other modal verbs to change the meaning. For instance, if you want to express a possibility rather than a certainty, use might have. For example: If we had left earlier, we might have caught the train. If you want to talk about ability or opportunity, use could have: If you had asked me, I could have helped you.

In formal English, such as professional presentations or academic essays, you can also use what is called inversion. To do this, simply drop the word if and put had before the subject. Instead of saying, If I had known about the traffic, you can say: Had I known about the traffic, I would have taken another route. It sounds very polished and elegant.

Before we finish, keep these common pitfalls in mind.

First, never put would have inside the if-clause. You will often hear native speakers make this mistake, but grammatically, it is incorrect. Do not say, if I would have known. Always say, if I had known.

Second, always use the past participle after had and would have. Be especially careful with irregular verbs. We say had seen, not had saw.

Third, never drop the auxiliary have. Saying she would passed is a common mistake; make sure you say she would have passed.

And finally, remember that the third conditional refers strictly to the unchangeable past, never to future possibilities. 

Try thinking of a choice you made yesterday, and say aloud what would have happened if you had done things differently. You are doing fantastic work, so keep practicing, and I will see you in our next lesson!
