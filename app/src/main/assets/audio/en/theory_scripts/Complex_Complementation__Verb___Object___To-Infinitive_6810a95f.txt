Hello there! Today, we are diving into a fascinating area of English grammar called complex transitive complementation. This sounds fancy, but it simply refers to sentences where a verb needs both an object and a to-infinitive to make sense.

The basic formula is straightforward: you have a subject, followed by a verb, then a direct object, and finally, a to-infinitive phrase.

Let’s look at some common categories. First, we have causative or permissive verbs like allow, permit, force, or compel. For example, you might say, She forced him to apologize. Notice the pronunciation of apologize—it is uh-POL-uh-jize. This structure shows that someone is being coerced or authorized to do something.

Next, we have cognitive or mental verbs like believe, consider, expect, or know. We use these to express an expectation or a mental assessment, such as, We expect them to arrive early.

Then, there are directive or interpersonal verbs like advise, ask, beg, encourage, invite, persuade, tell, or urge. These involve persuasion or instruction. For instance, I advised her to accept the job offer.

Finally, we have verbs of desire or intent, like want, would like, prefer, or intend. These express what you want someone else to do, like when you say, They want us to finish the project today.

Now, how do we handle these in the passive voice? It is quite simple. The direct object becomes the new subject. For example, if the active sentence is, The manager invited the client to inspect the facility, the passive version becomes, The client was invited to inspect the facility. Notice how the to-infinitive remains unchanged.

Before we wrap up, let’s watch out for a few common pitfalls. First, don't forget the object! If you want someone else to do something, you must include them in the sentence. Don't just say I want to win if you actually mean I want him to win.

Second, remember that these verbs take a to-infinitive, not a gerund. So, say she forced him to apologize, not she forced him apologizing.

Third, avoid mixing these up with that-clauses. It is incorrect to say I advised that she to go. Just stick to the direct object and the to-infinitive.

Lastly, don't forget the particle to. Unlike the verbs make or let, which take a bare infinitive, verbs like permit or allow always need that to. So, always say they permitted him to enter, never they permitted him enter.

Keep practicing these patterns, and you will sound more natural and precise in no time. Great work today!
