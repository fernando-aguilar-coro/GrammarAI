Hello there! Today, we are diving into defining relative clauses. These are essential tools in English that help us identify exactly which person, animal, or thing we are talking about. Without them, our sentences would often feel incomplete or confusing.

Let’s start with the relative pronouns. Think of these as the bridges connecting your noun to the extra information. When talking about people, we use who. For example, the woman who called me is a doctor. When referring to things or animals, we use which, like in the sentence, the laptop which I bought is fast. You can also use that for people, things, or animals, such as the car that crashed was red. Finally, we use whose to show possession, as in the artist whose painting won is local.

There are two important rules to remember regarding subject and object functions. If the relative pronoun is the subject of the clause, you must include it. For instance, you must say the man who lives next door is loud. However, if the pronoun is the object, you can often leave it out. You could say the book that I read was fascinating, or simply, the book I read was fascinating.

A quick tip on the word that: it is very versatile, but it has limits. You cannot use that after a comma or immediately after a preposition. So, we say the house in which she lives, never the house in that she lives.

Also, be careful with whose. It replaces words like his, her, or their, and it must always be followed by a noun. Don't confuse it with who’s, which is a contraction for who is or who has.

Finally, watch out for these common pitfalls. First, never use commas with defining relative clauses because they are essential to the meaning of the sentence. Second, remember that you cannot omit the relative pronoun if it is the subject of the clause. And third, always use who or that for people, never which.

Practice these patterns, and you will find your sentences becoming much more precise and natural. Keep up the great work!
