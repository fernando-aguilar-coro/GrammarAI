Hello there! Welcome to today's English lesson. Today, we are going to master descriptive adjectives, pronounced as de-scrip-tive ad-jec-tives, and explore how they bring our language to life through our five senses. 

Descriptive adjectives paint vivid mental pictures by appealing directly to what we see, hear, smell, taste, and touch. Let's look at some beautiful examples for each of these senses. 

For sight, we have visual words like vivid, gigantic, or spherical. For sound, think of auditory words like cacophonous, whispering, or shrill. When it comes to smell, we can use olfactory words like fragrant, pungent, or musty. For taste, we might describe something as bitter, savory, or luscious. And finally, for touch, we have tactile words like velvety, abrasive, or viscous. 

Now, what happens when we want to use more than one adjective? In English, we follow a very specific order. You might have heard of the acronym OSASCOMP, which stands for Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Color, Origin, Material, and Purpose. For sensory adjectives, this means we usually describe size, shape, age, and color in that specific order before the noun. 

We can place these adjectives in two main positions. First is the attributive position, which is directly before the noun. For example: The velvety fabric draped over the chair. Second is the predicative position, which comes after a linking verb like be, seem, appear, feel, look, or sound. For example: The fabric feels velvety. 

To sound like a native speaker, let's watch out for a few common pitfalls. 

First, remember to use commas between adjectives of the same type. Write "a cold, dark night" with a comma, not just "a cold dark night." 

Second, make sure you follow the correct adjective order. We say "a huge red box," not "a red huge box." 

Third, be careful with "-ing" and "-ed" adjectives. The "-ing" form describes the source of the feeling, while the "-ed" form describes how someone feels. So, we say "the food was disgusting," not "the food was disgusted." 

Lastly, avoid pairing absolute adjectives with the word "very." Instead of saying "very freezing," simply say "freezing." 

Keep practicing these sensory details, and you will see your English become much more expressive and natural. Happy learning, and I will see you in our next lesson!
