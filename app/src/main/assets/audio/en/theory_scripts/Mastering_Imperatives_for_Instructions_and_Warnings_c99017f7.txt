Hello and welcome! Today, we are going to master a very important part of English grammar: the imperative mood. We use imperatives every day to give clear instructions, share warnings, and make polite requests. 

The best thing about the imperative is how simple it is. In English, we form the imperative by using the base form of the verb, without any subject. The subject "you" is already implied, so you do not need to say it.

Let us look at three common ways we structure imperatives.

First, for a direct, affirmative instruction, we use the base verb followed by a complement. For example, to tell someone to open a door, we say: Open the door. When you pronounce this, try to link the words smoothly: open the door. 

Second, for a negative warning, we use do not, or the contraction don't, followed by the base verb. For instance, if there is a dangerous electrical hazard, you would say: Don't touch that wire. Listen to how the words flow together: Don't touch that wire. 

Third, if you want to make a polite request, simply add the word please before the base verb. For example, you can say: Please sign here. Pronounce it clearly and softly: Please sign here.

Imperatives are incredibly useful, especially in professional settings, safety manuals, and emergency situations. When safety is on the line, using strong negative imperatives like "don't" or "never" can prevent serious hazards.

Now, let us look at a few common mistakes to avoid so you can speak with confidence.

First, try not to include the pronoun "you." Many learners say, "You close the window." To make it a proper imperative, just drop the "you" and say, "Close the window."

Second, avoid using the infinitive or the "ing" form of the verb. Instead of saying, "To write your name" or "Writing your name," always use the simple base form: "Write your name."

Third, be careful with where you place the negative word. We never say, "Do touch not." It must always be, "Don't touch," or more formally, "Do not touch."

Finally, keep in mind that while imperatives are perfect for instructions and emergencies, they can sound a bit too direct or even bossy in casual social situations. When speaking to colleagues or customers, remember to soften your commands by adding "please," or by rephrasing them as polite questions.

With these simple rules in mind, you are ready to give clear, confident instructions in English. Keep practicing, and I will see you in our next lesson!
