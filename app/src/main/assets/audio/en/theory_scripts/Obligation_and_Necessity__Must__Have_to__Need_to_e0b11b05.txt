Hello everyone, and welcome to today’s English lesson! Today, we are going to master how to express obligation and necessity using must, have to, and need to.

Let’s start with expressing strong obligation using must and have to. While both words express a strong requirement, the difference comes down to where that obligation comes from. 

We use must when the obligation comes from inside ourselves, such as a personal conviction or our own authority. For example, you might say, "I must finish this report today."

On the other hand, we use have to when the rule comes from an external source, like a law, workplace requirement, or a boss. For instance, "Employees have to wear safety gear." Notice how in natural spoken English, have to is often pronounced like "haf-too."

Now, pay very close attention to the negative forms, because changing these words to negative completely changes their meaning!

When you say "don't have to," it means there is a lack of necessity. The action is purely optional. For example: "You don't have to attend the meeting." This means you are welcome to go, but you do not need to.

However, "mustn't"—which is pronounced with a silent middle t as "muss-ent"—means strict prohibition. It means something is strictly forbidden! For example: "You mustn't park in front of the hydrant."

Next, let's look at "need to." This phrase expresses a requirement and behaves very similarly to "have to." For instance, "We need to submit the documents by Friday." To express a lack of necessity in the negative, you can say, "You don't need to worry about the costs," or in more formal British English, "You needn't worry about the costs."

Before we finish, let's cover four very common mistakes to avoid.

First, never say "musted" for the past tense. The correct past form for both must and have to is always "had to."

Second, don't confuse "mustn't" with "don't have to." Remember, "mustn't" means it is forbidden, while "don't have to" means it is optional.

Third, make sure not to drop the word "to" after have and need. Always say "I have to finish," rather than "I have finish."

And finally, native speakers rarely ask questions using "Must I?" Instead, they almost always ask, "Do I have to?"
